The Windfall Clause: Trigger, Administration, and Enforcement

A precise walkthrough of AGI Future Foundation PBC's Windfall Clause — how the profit threshold is set, who administers the redistribution, and what legal and governance mechanisms give the clause teeth.

Why a Windfall Clause Exists

The economic logic of transformative technology concentrates gains. The entities that own the technology, hold the equity, or control the platform capture most of the value; the rest of the world receives diffuse, hard-to-attribute benefits that arrive unevenly and often lag by years. This is a familiar pattern in the history of general-purpose technologies — electricity, the internal combustion engine, the commercial internet — and it is manageable when the technology in question improves productivity at the margin.

Artificial general intelligence is not a marginal improvement. A system capable of performing substantially all economically valuable cognitive tasks would represent a discontinuity in the history of value creation. Economic models of such a transition — and the serious ones acknowledge enormous uncertainty — suggest that the distributional consequences could dwarf anything produced by prior technological shifts. The gains to equity holders in the organizations that develop and deploy such systems could be extraordinary; the dislocation to those whose labor is displaced could be severe and abrupt.

AGI Future Foundation PBC (the "Foundation") takes the position that this distributional outcome is not a background condition to be managed by governments after the fact but a design problem to be addressed at the level of the enterprise. The Windfall Clause is the mechanism by which the Foundation embeds a redistribution commitment directly into its operating governance — not as a policy aspiration but as a structural feature of how profits above a defined threshold are handled.

This article explains the Windfall Clause with the specificity that investor diligence requires: how the trigger is defined, how the administration mechanism is intended to function, and what gives the clause enforcement teeth in a legal structure that must remain commercially viable.

*Note: The specific legal instruments implementing the Windfall Clause are in development. The following describes the intended structure and mechanism. Nothing herein constitutes legal advice; all mechanisms should be evaluated with the assistance of qualified counsel familiar with PBC governance, contractual charity commitments, and applicable tax law.*

Defining the Trigger: Threshold Architecture

The Windfall Clause activates when the Foundation's operating group — primarily through its AGI development subsidiary, OGI — generates profits above a defined threshold in a given measurement period. The threshold is designed to accomplish two things: it must be high enough that it does not impede normal commercial reinvestment during the development phase, and it must be specific enough that it cannot be engineered around through accounting choices.

The Profit Metric

The triggering metric is intended to be a version of distributable profit — roughly, net income after taxes and after amounts set aside for defined reinvestment categories, but before discretionary distributions to equity holders. The precise definition matters enormously. A clause that triggers on gross revenue is too blunt and could impede operations before meaningful windfall has been achieved. A clause that triggers on GAAP net income is vulnerable to aggressive accounting treatment of capital expenditures, depreciation schedules, and intangible amortization — all of which could be used to suppress the reported figure while cash generation remains substantial.

The Foundation's intended approach is to define the trigger against a cash-based or near-cash metric, with specific carve-outs for defined reinvestment categories (safety research, alignment evaluation, infrastructure essential to safe operations) and specific prohibitions on carve-outs that could be used to obscure distributable profit (management fees to affiliates above arm's-length rates, discretionary equity repurchases, compensation above defined bands for defined personnel).

*Illustrative example: if OGI generates $10 billion in a measurement year, with $2 billion in defined reinvestment (safety research, alignment infrastructure) and $1 billion in taxes, the triggering profit figure might be $7 billion. If the threshold is set at $5 billion, the clause activates, and the excess — $2 billion — is subject to redistribution. The specific numbers are illustrative; actual thresholds and carve-out definitions will be established by the Foundation's governing documents.*

Threshold Setting and Adjustment

The initial threshold is intended to be set in the Foundation's governing documents at a level that reflects reasonable projections of the investment required to sustain development and maintain commercial viability. The Foundation's position is that the threshold should be set conservatively — meaning the clause should activate meaningfully if AGI-level capability is achieved, not merely if the development subsidiary becomes profitable.

Because the economic environment in which AGI operates cannot be precisely anticipated, the governing documents are intended to include a threshold adjustment mechanism: the threshold may be adjusted upward by a defined fraction of CPI or a defined proxy for general price levels in each measurement period, subject to a cap on cumulative adjustment. Adjustments require approval by the Foundation's board, which includes members whose mandate is specifically to represent the public benefit purpose rather than equity holder returns. Any upward adjustment of the threshold above the CPI adjustment cap requires a supermajority of the board, including a majority of the public-purpose members.

Administration: Who Decides Where the Money Goes

A redistribution commitment is only as credible as its administration. If the organization that generates the windfall also decides unilaterally where the redistribution goes, the commitment is functionally a discretionary philanthropy program — valuable, but not structurally distinct from a standard corporate foundation that can be defunded in the next board cycle.

The Foundation's intended Windfall Clause structure separates the generation of the redistributable amount from its administration through an independent governance layer.

The Distribution Council

The Foundation's intended structure includes a Distribution Council — a standing body with defined membership criteria, defined decision-making procedures, and defined authority over Windfall Clause distributions. The Distribution Council is intended to operate independently of the Foundation's commercial board; its members serve staggered terms, are appointed by a combination of the Foundation's public-purpose board members and external nominees designated by qualifying public interest organizations, and cannot be removed by the commercial board.

The Distribution Council's mandate is to direct Windfall Clause distributions toward uses that advance broad human welfare, with specific guidance that distributions should prioritize populations and regions most affected by AGI-driven economic dislocation. The Council is not a grant-making foundation in the traditional sense; it does not solicit proposals from grantees. It is a governance body that directs transfers to established distribution mechanisms — existing international development institutions, sovereign wealth-adjacent mechanisms, direct-transfer programs with auditable delivery infrastructure — selected on the basis of administrative efficiency and distributional reach.

*Illustrative example: in a year in which the Windfall Clause activates at the $2 billion level described above, the Distribution Council might direct 60% to a defined set of international development institutions (illustratively, multilateral development banks or UN-affiliated bodies with established transfer capacity), 30% to direct-transfer programs targeting regions with documented AGI-related labor displacement, and 10% to a reserve fund for future Distribution Council operations and administration. These allocations are illustrative; actual allocation criteria will be established in the Distribution Council's charter.*

Transparency and Audit Requirements

Windfall Clause distributions are subject to annual audit by an independent auditor separate from the Foundation's commercial auditor. The audit covers both the computation of the redistributable amount (confirming that the triggering metric has been accurately calculated and that carve-outs have been properly applied) and the disbursement of distributed funds (confirming that directed transfers have reached their intended recipients). Audit results are published on the Foundation's website within 90 days of fiscal year end.

This transparency regime serves two functions. First, it creates a public accountability mechanism that is independent of the Foundation's governance structure — if distributions are understated or misdirected, the discrepancy will appear in the audit record. Second, it provides investors with a documented basis for assessing whether the Windfall Clause is operating as intended, which is material information for any equity holder evaluating the Foundation's compliance with its stated mission.

Enforcement: What Gives the Clause Teeth

A contractual redistribution commitment faces an obvious enforcement problem: if the organization that made the commitment decides not to honor it, the question is who can sue, in what court, and for what remedy. The Foundation's intended structure addresses this through overlapping enforcement layers.

PBC Statutory Standing

California's Public Benefit Corporation Law provides standing for the California Attorney General, and in some circumstances for members with qualifying interests, to bring enforcement actions against a PBC that operates contrary to its stated public benefit purpose. Because the Windfall Clause is embedded in the Foundation's articles of incorporation as a component of its public benefit purpose — not merely as a contractual term in a separate agreement — violation of the clause is intended to constitute a violation of the PBC's organizing purpose, triggering the Attorney General's enforcement jurisdiction.

This is a meaningful deterrent. A California Attorney General enforcement action against a PBC for failing to honor its redistribution commitment is categorically different from a breach-of-contract action between private parties. It is public, reputationally costly, and carries remedies that include removal of directors and court supervision of the organization's operations.

*The extent to which the Attorney General's enforcement authority would apply in specific circumstances depends on interpretations of California law that have not been definitively resolved. Investors should obtain independent legal advice on this point.*

Contractual Reinforcement

The Windfall Clause commitment is also intended to be embedded in the commercial contracts between the Foundation and OGI's operating subsidiaries. Specifically, the development and deployment licenses under which OGI's subsidiaries operate are intended to include Windfall Clause compliance as a condition of the license. A subsidiary that fails to remit the redistributable amount in accordance with the clause's terms is in breach of its operating license, which triggers remedies up to and including license termination.

This creates a commercial enforcement pathway that operates in parallel with the PBC enforcement pathway. It is designed to make non-compliance costly not only in terms of legal liability but in terms of operational continuity — a subsidiary that loses its development license cannot continue operations.

Governance Enforcement

The Foundation's dual-class share structure — with Class C shares carrying 1,000 votes per share held by the founder governance layer — means that the board composition required to approve any amendment to the Windfall Clause is effectively controlled by parties whose governance mandate includes honoring the redistribution commitment. Any attempt to weaken or eliminate the Windfall Clause through a board vote would require Class C approval, and Class C holders are bound by governance documents that make protection of the public benefit purpose a condition of their voting rights.

This is not an unlimited protection — governance structures can evolve, and no document is perfectly amendment-proof — but it means that weakening the Windfall Clause is not a decision that can be made by commercial equity holders acting alone, regardless of how much Class A capital they hold.

What the Windfall Clause Does Not Do

Sophisticated investors deserve an honest accounting of the limits of the mechanism.

The Windfall Clause does not create a right for third parties — individuals, governments, or international organizations — to sue the Foundation for non-compliance. Third-party beneficiary standing in this context is uncertain, and the Foundation's intended structure does not rely on it as a primary enforcement mechanism.

The Windfall Clause does not prevent the Foundation from reinvesting profits below the threshold. It is not a cap on commercial success; it is a condition attached to extraordinary success. The distinction matters for investors: Class A profit participation rights are not compromised by the Windfall Clause in the range of outcomes that a traditional commercial venture would regard as successful. The clause activates in the range of outcomes that only become available if AGI-level capability is achieved.

The Windfall Clause does not specify the precise distribution mechanism for all scenarios. The Distribution Council has discretion within defined parameters, and its decisions may not satisfy every stakeholder's view of optimal redistribution. The Foundation's position is that distributional optimality is not achievable with certainty; what the structure achieves is distributional commitment — the assurance that redistribution happens, at meaningful scale, through accountable processes.

Investor Implications

For investors evaluating Class A profit participation, the Windfall Clause has two direct financial implications.

First, it reduces the upside available to Class A holders in the scenario of extraordinary commercial success. If AGI-level capability generates profits substantially above the threshold, a meaningful fraction of those profits — the redistributable amount — flows to the Distribution Council rather than to equity holders. This is not a hidden feature; it is a disclosed and deliberate design choice. Investors who are seeking maximum exposure to AGI upside should evaluate whether the Windfall Clause's trigger level and redistribution fraction are acceptable given their return requirements.

Second, it is part of the bundle of commitments that makes the Foundation's safety-first operating posture credible and durable. An organization that has embedded a redistribution commitment in its legal structure, governance documents, and operating licenses is demonstrating a level of institutional commitment to its mission that is material to any assessment of long-term organizational stability. Investors who value that stability — because they believe organizations with credible safety commitments will be better positioned to navigate the regulatory and reputational environment that AGI development will attract — may view the Windfall Clause as value-positive rather than value-negative.
